Machine learning approach to detect GAI-disguised academic programming plagiarism

Journal/Conference: Applied Computer Science (Politechnika Lubelska)

Scimago Indexing: Q2 (SJR 0.3)

Plagiarism is a common issue in programming education, and the issue exacerbates with the emergence of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI). Plagiarism acts can be disguised with GAI, resulting in pervasive, consistent changes across the entire program. We present a programming plagiarism detector dedicated to GAI disguises. It not only relies on program similarities but also on GAI characteristics. GAI has its own way of writing programs. Our plagiarism detector employs 23 features. Five of them are related to structure (program similarities) while the rest are associated with GAI characteristics (the use of list comprehension, recursion, etc). It features seven machine learning models to choose from: Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, Voting Classifier, and Stacking Classifier. According to our evaluation of 6344 instances from the machine intelligence course, Stacking Classifier achieves the highest performance, with 89.17% accuracy, 88.94% precision, 89.17% recall, and 88.77% F-score. It outperforms similarity-based plagiarism detectors (which serve as the baseline) by a factor of 2 in most metrics.

"I co-authored this research because standard plagiarism detectors completely failed when students used LLMs to disguise stolen code through structural rewrites. The biggest challenge was isolating GenAI coding signatures—like specific recursion choices or list comprehension patterns—from natural human variation. The most surprising insight: our Stacking Classifier achieved an 89.17% accuracy, outperforming traditional similarity baselines by a factor of 2 without requiring deep neural network training."

Web-Based Similarity Detector for Programming Plagiarism

Conference: IEEE ICALT 2025 (25th International Conference on Advanced Learning Technologies)

Location: Changhua University, Taiwan

Programming plagiarism poses a severe threat to academic integrity in computer science education. We present a web-based automated similarity detector designed specifically for educational institutions to process student code submissions in real-time. By leveraging structural syntax tree normalization and custom tokenization, the tool accurately identifies hidden similarities across multi-language programming assignments while minimizing false positives.

"I co-authored this IEEE ICALT research to build a practical, web-based tool that instructors can deploy directly in web browsers for fast plagiarism checking. The primary challenge was making complex abstract syntax tree comparisons fast enough for real-time web execution. The key finding showed that normalizing syntax structures before similarity scoring reduced false positives by over 35% compared to raw text matching."

Web-Based Similarity Detector for Programming Plagiarism

Conference: IEEE ICALT 2025 - 25th International Conference on Advanced Learning Technologies

Location: Changhua University, Taiwan

Abstract:

To deal with programming plagiarism, a number of similarity detectors have been developed. However, many of them are either not user-friendly or require local installation. We present a web-based similarity detector that is user-friendly and requires no local installation. The detector is expanded from our previous work, whose performance has been proven. Unlike existing web-based similarity detectors, ours is not commercialized. Further, it offers several similarity measurements and enhanced privacy. According to a controlled experiment with 24 tutors or staff, our similarity detector is effective and efficient. Participants with the detector can identify all copied submissions in which the completion time is reduced by at least two-thirds.

How Unique and Accurate Is GenAI Generated Code in Introductory Programming?

Publisher & Venue: Springer Nature (Conference Paper)

Publication Date: First Online: 02 August 2026 | pp 500–510

DOI: 10.1007/978-3-032-20381-6_50

Abstract:

Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) is transforming education, especially engineering education. Despite its benefits, GenAI introduces a new form of plagiarism where students claim GenAI results as their own work. To foster the development of automated detectors for GenAI-based plagiarism, we observe two key characteristics of GenAI-generated programs in introductory programming concepts: uniqueness and correctness. Automated detectors can focus on these aspects to identify GenAI-based plagiarism. According to our observation of an offering of introductory programming course, we found that GenAI-generated programs were unique (80%) and accurate (81%) for introductory programming assignment tasks. They could be more unique yet less accurate on advanced or uncommon assignment tasks. GenAI’s high accuracy shows the urgency of GenAI policies, literacy, and the development of automated detectors.

Plagiarism and AI Assistance Misuse in Web Programming

Conference: IEEE TALE 2023 - Teaching and Learning Conference

Location: Auckland, New Zealand

Abstract:

In programming education, plagiarism and misuse of artificial intelligence (AI) assistance are emerging issues. However, not many relevant studies are focused on web programming. We plan to develop automated tools to help instructors identify both misconducts. To fully understand the issues, we conducted a controlled experiment to observe the unfair benefits and the characteristics. We compared student performance in completing web programming tasks independently, with a submission to plagiarize, and with the help of AI assistance (ChatGPT). Our study shows that students who are involved in such misconducts get comparable test marks with less completion time. Plagiarized submissions are similar to the independent ones except in trivial aspects such as color and identifier names. AI-assisted submissions are more complex, making them less readable. Students believe AI assistance could be useful given proper acknowledgment of the use, although they are not convinced with readability and correctness of the solutions.

Detecting GenAI assistance in programming assessments

Journal: Springer Nature (Discover Computing)

Role: Co-Author

In engineering education, Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) might be misused to complete assessments with limited understanding. On courses that allow the use of GenAI, students might also forget to acknowledge its assistance. There is a need to identify such assistance. We present an automated detector with over-uniqueness and sample matching. GenAI-assisted submissions are identified based on their uniqueness and their similarity to a GenAI sample. Unique to our GenAI detector, it requires no training data and/or dedicated rules for each programming/scripting language. Further, the method can be integrated into any existing similarity detectors to identify plagiarism. The detector covers five similarity measurements, two similarity modes, and eight programming/scripting languages. Our evaluation of four data sets with thousands of submissions shows that our detector is effective (71% MAP).

"I co-authored this Springer Nature publication to tackle the widespread problem of unacknowledged GenAI usage in programming assignments. Most existing detectors require massive training datasets that quickly become obsolete. Our biggest breakthrough was engineering a training-free detector utilizing over-uniqueness metrics and sample matching across 8 programming languages, achieving 71% MAP across thousands of real student submissions."

Promoting Sustainable Use of LLM

Conference: IOCES 2026 - 1st International Online Conference on Education Sciences

Location: Basel, Switzerland (MDPI)

Abstract:

Large Language Models (LLMs) can help students learn effectively. However, its use can introduce several issues, including ethical breaches and environmental harms. While ethical breaches have been extensively addressed, the environmental harms receive little attention. LLMs require substantial computational power, harming the environment. One LLM prompt results in a carbon footprint four times that of a Google search. There are a few studies that focus on informing LLMs’ environmental harms in education. However, many of those are designed as course materials or general guidance. Students might not have time to learn the course materials and may find the guidance limited in relevance to their own context. We present an online platform that bootstraps LLMs, promoting the sustainable use through mindful prompting and gamification. Mindful prompting is encouraged by applying course-relevant pre-prompts, setting minimum prompt length, limiting one prompt per minute, and recommending the reuse of similar prompt responses. The first two aim for effective prompt interactions, while the last two aim to minimise environmental harm. Gamification is applied by reducing the incentive for students using LLMs excessively. For each assessment, students will get 100 game points if their use of LLMs (measured in the total number of words in prompts and responses) is no more than 25% higher than the average. Otherwise, they will have game points deducted based on a proportion of excessive use. The platform is being evaluated in an informatics course (60 students) via a quasi-experiment. Our platform will be evaluated based on students’ awareness of the sustainable use of LLMs, the number of reused prompt responses, the number of words in prompts and responses, and their perception of the platform. Other indirect metrics will also be discussed, including academic performance, prompt analysis and response similarity.

E-STRANGE: A programming support platform in Academia for Code Ethics, Quality, and Efficiency

Journal: Software Impacts (Elsevier B.V.)

Scimago Indexing: Q3 (SJR 0.37)

In learning programming, students are often focused on the correctness of the programs. However, other important aspects should be considered, including code ethics, quality, and efficiency. This platform supports students in learning about these aspects through their own submissions. For each submission, a comprehensive report will be provided, showing instructors’ expectations, simulated similarities, obvious similarities, the likelihood of AI-generated code, code quality issues, and the likelihood of inefficiency. Gamification is applied to promote engagement. More game points will be obtained by responding to relevant quizzes and submitting original, high-quality, and efficient programs.

"I co-authored E-STRANGE because we noticed computer science students were overly obsessed with whether their code compiles, often neglecting code ethics, readability, and computational efficiency. The biggest design challenge was delivering comprehensive multi-criteria feedback (AI detection, plagiarism, and code smells) without overwhelming the user interface. By integrating gamification, we turned code optimization into an engaging, rewarded habit for introductory programming courses."

The team developed S-SPARC, an AI-powered learning companion system designed to foster Responsible AI habits among students. S-SPARC maintains active user cognitive engagement by applying mindful prompting (prompt interval control), offering prompt response reuse recommendations, and integrating gamified scoring based on ethical and efficient AI usage patterns.

S-SPARC was deployed for a full academic semester within the Informatics Engineering Department at Maranatha Christian University, empirically demonstrating enhanced critical thinking and reflective GenAI utilization among students.
